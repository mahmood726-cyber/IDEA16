# Are ICD Meta-Analyses Obsolete? Lessons from the Aspirin Reversal

## The Aspirin Precedent: A Cautionary Tale

In 2009, a landmark meta-analysis recommended aspirin for primary prevention of cardiovascular disease based on trials from the 1980s-2000s. The evidence seemed robust: a 12% relative risk reduction in serious vascular events, derived from nearly 100,000 patients across six trials. Guidelines incorporated this recommendation. Millions of patients started taking daily aspirin.

**The meta-analysis was not wrong. It was obsolete.**

By 2018, three large trials tested aspirin in contemporary populations already on statins and modern blood pressure medications. ARRIVE, ASCEND, and ASPREE—conducted in over 45,000 patients—were all concordantly negative. The baseline risk of cardiovascular events had fallen so substantially due to statin therapy that aspirin's 12% relative risk reduction no longer produced meaningful absolute benefit. Meanwhile, bleeding risks remained unchanged. The risk-benefit ratio had reversed.

Within months, guidelines reversed. Aspirin for primary prevention, once standard of care, is now recommended against in most populations.

**This exact scenario may be repeating itself with implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) in non-ischemic cardiomyopathy.**

## The ICD Story: An Unsettling Parallel

Current guidelines recommend ICDs for primary prevention in patients with non-ischemic cardiomyopathy (NICM) and left ventricular ejection fraction ≤35% based on meta-analyses from the 1990s-2000s. These trials—CAT, DEFINITE, AMIOVIRT, and SCD-HeFT—demonstrated a 23% reduction in sudden cardiac death, with meta-analysis confirming benefit.

But these trials were conducted in a different therapeutic era. Patients received ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers, often at suboptimal doses. Three game-changing therapies didn't exist:

- **Angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNi)**: Approved 2015, reduce sudden death
- **SGLT2 inhibitors**: Approved 2019-2020 for heart failure, reduce arrhythmic deaths
- **Modern cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT)**: Optimized algorithms, reduce sudden death

The baseline sudden cardiac death rate in those trials was 8-10% over two years. **That baseline no longer exists.**

## The Evidence: A 44% Decline in Sudden Death

A 2017 New England Journal of Medicine analysis examined 40,195 patients from 12 heart failure trials spanning 1995-2014. The findings were striking: annual sudden cardiac death rates fell from 6.5% in 1998 to 3.3% in 2014—a 44% relative decline (P=0.02).

This represents a drop from approximately 13% to 6.6% over two years. Critically, the 2014 endpoint preceded widespread SGLT2 inhibitor use, suggesting current rates may be even lower.

Registry data confirms this trend. The Swedish Heart Failure Registry documented progressive reductions in sudden death from 2002 to 2018, with sudden death accounting for a decreasing proportion of total deaths.

**The population studied in ICD meta-analyses is vanishing.**

## The Math: When Benefit Evaporates

Here's where epidemiology becomes clinical reality. If ICDs provide a constant 23% relative risk reduction but baseline risk has fallen, the absolute benefit shrinks:

**Historical trials (1998):**
- Baseline sudden death risk: 13% (RALES, pre-modern therapy)
- Absolute risk reduction: 13% × 0.23 = 3.0%
- Number needed to treat (NNT): **33 patients**

**Partial GDMT era (2014, PARADIGM-HF):**
- Baseline sudden death risk: 6.6% (ARNi available, SGLT2i not yet standard)
- Absolute risk reduction: 6.6% × 0.23 = 1.5%
- Number needed to treat: **66 patients**

**Full GDMT era (2020+, estimated):**
- Baseline sudden death risk: 4.6-5.6% (ARNi + SGLT2i both standard)
- Absolute risk reduction: 1.1-1.3%
- Number needed to treat: **78-94 patients**

The NNT has doubled—and may triple. Meanwhile, the harms remain constant: device infections, lead failures, inappropriate shocks affect approximately 1 in 30 patients regardless of when the ICD is implanted.

**The risk-benefit ratio has deteriorated from 1.1:1 (highly favorable) to 2.6-3.1:1 (unfavorable).**

## The Proof: DANISH and the Disappearing Benefit

If this analysis seems speculative, consider DANISH (2016), the only large ICD trial conducted in a partially modern era. This trial enrolled 1,116 NICM patients from 2008-2014, before ARNi and SGLT2i became available but with 58% receiving CRT and 96% on contemporary medical therapy.

**Results:**
- ICDs reduced sudden cardiac death from 8.2% to 4.3% (HR 0.50, P<0.01)—a clear win
- But overall mortality showed no benefit: 23.4% versus 21.6% (HR 0.87, P=0.28)—not significant

**Why the disconnect?** Competing risks. Patients saved from sudden death died from other causes instead—progressive heart failure, cancer, other cardiovascular events. Non-sudden cardiac deaths actually increased from 15.2% to 17.3% in the ICD group.

DANISH is to ICDs what ARRIVE, ASCEND, and ASPREE were to aspirin: a trial in a contemporary population showing that the benefit from historical meta-analyses has vanished.

And DANISH was conducted **before** ARNi and SGLT2i became standard. The benefit today is likely smaller still.

## What Should We Do?

Approximately 40,000-50,000 ICDs are implanted annually in the United States for primary prevention in NICM, at costs exceeding $30,000 per device. If these recommendations rest on obsolete evidence, we may be exposing tens of thousands of patients to device harms with diminished or absent benefit.

Three actions are needed:

**1. New randomized trials in the GDMT era**
We need trials enrolling patients on ARNi, SGLT2i, and optimized medical therapy—the standard of care today. Powering these trials for modern baseline sudden death rates (not the historical 8-10%) is essential.

**2. Optimize medical therapy first**
Before considering ICDs, ensure patients receive maximal guideline-directed therapy for 3-6 months. Up to 40% of NICM patients show meaningful LVEF improvement on modern GDMT, obviating ICD need.

**3. Honest shared decision-making**
Tell patients the truth: "Historical trials showed ICDs prevent sudden death in 1 out of 33-54 patients. A modern trial found no survival benefit. Your medications are better than those trials tested. We estimate the benefit today may be 1 in 66-94 patients. Device complications still affect 1 in 30. Some patients still benefit, but we can't identify who."

## The Lesson from Aspirin

The aspirin reversal teaches us that **meta-analyses become obsolete when the populations we treat today differ fundamentally from the populations studied in trials**. The relative risk reduction may remain constant, but when baseline risk falls dramatically, absolute benefit evaporates while harms persist.

It took a decade for the medical community to recognize aspirin's obsolescence—from the 2009 meta-analysis to the 2019 guideline reversal. During that decade, millions of patients received marginal or negative benefit.

**We should not repeat that mistake with ICDs.**

DANISH was published in 2016. It's now 2025. The signal is clear: in contemporary populations with modern medical therapy, ICD mortality benefit has disappeared. Yet guidelines remain unchanged, and thousands of devices are implanted monthly based on data from a therapeutic era that no longer exists.

The aspirin story proves this isn't speculation—it's a pattern we've seen before. The question is whether we'll recognize it faster this time.
